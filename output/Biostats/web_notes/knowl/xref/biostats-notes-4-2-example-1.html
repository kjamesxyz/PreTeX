Example 4.2.2.

In a survey of 36 randomly selected senior citizens, 28 reported frequently using social media. Build a 95% confidence interval for the proportion of all senior citizens that frequently use social media.
🔗
Solution.
Recall the goal is to find \(a,b\) such that we are 95% confident that \(p \in [a,b]\text{.}\)
🔗
Since \(n \geq 30 \text{,}\) we can apply the Central Limit Theorem. That is, we know the sampling distribution for \(\hat{p}\) is normal with mean \(p\) and standard error \(\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \text{.}\) We do not actually know \(p\text{,}\) so we will have to use our point estimate
\begin{equation*} \hat{p} = \frac{28}{36} \approx 0.7778. \end{equation*}
This gives us the approximation that
\begin{equation*} \mu_{\hat{p}} \approx 0.2 \ \text{and} \ \sigma_{\hat{p}} \approx 0.0667. \end{equation*}
🔗
To capture 95% of the data, we need to find how many standard errors to move in each direction. This number of standard errors is what we call the margin of error for the interval. It is the Z-score \(Z^*\) that gives
\begin{equation*} \cal{P}[|Z| \geq Z^*] = (1-\alpha)% = 95%. \text{.} \end{equation*}
You’ll notice that we have "5% error" to split between evenly between the two tails on our diagram. By symmetry we can just find \(Z^*\) such that
\begin{equation*} \cal{P}[Z \leq Z^*] = \frac{\alpha}{2}. \end{equation*}
Using the R command qnorm(0.025) gives
\begin{equation*} Z^* = \pm 1.96. \end{equation*}
Hence, the margin of error is
\begin{equation*} ME = |Z^*| \cdot \sigma_{\hat{p}} = 1.96 \cdot 0.0667 = 0.1307. \end{equation*}
🔗
We are done with the logic here. If our point estimate was the true proportion, then 95% of samples would find a proportion within our margin of error from our parameter. That is, the mean proportion of seniors that frequently use social media falls between
\begin{equation*} [\hat{p} - ME, \hat{p} + ME] = [0.6471, 0.9085]. \end{equation*}
