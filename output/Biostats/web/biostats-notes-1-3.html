    Section 1.3 Describing Quantitative Data

    Objectives

    Students will be able to...
    🔗
    1. Compute measures of location.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute measures of center.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Compute measures of spread.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Measures of Location

    Definition 1.3.1.

    A measure of location is a value that describes a datum’s location within a data set.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.2.

    The proportion \(p\) of values of \(X\) that are less than a datum \(x\) is called the percentile for \(x\text{.}\) We say that \(x\) is in the \(p^{\text{th}}\) percentile.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.3.

    The value of \(X\) that falls in the \((\frac{1}{q})^{\text{th}}\) percentile is called the \(q^{\text{th}}\) quantile for \(X\text{.}\) In the event of a tie between two data, \(q\) is equal to their mean.
    🔗
    🔗
    Quantiles can be computed in R with quantile(x,percent).
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.4.

    The quantiles associated with the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles are called the first, second, and third quartile respectively.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.5.

    Let \(x_{\text{min}}\) denote the smallest datum and \(x_{\text{max}}\) denote the larget datum. The five-number summary for a variable \(X\) is the list \(\{x_{\text{min}}, Q_1, Q_2, Q_3, x_{\text{max}}\} \text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Measures of Center

    Definition 1.3.6.

    A measure of center is a value that describes a middling/average value of a data set.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.7.

    The mean value of a variable \(X\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n}. \end{equation*}
    We denote it \(\bar{x}\) when \(X\) comes from a sample and \(\mu\) when it represents a population.
    🔗
    🔗
    Means can be computed in R with mean(x).
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.8.

    Consider ordering all values of a variable \(X\) from least to greatest. The median of \(X\) is the second quartile of \(X\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.9.

    The mode value of a variable \(X\) is the value it attains most frequently.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.10.

    The midrange value of a variable \(X\) is the mean of its minimum and maximum.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Measures of Spread

    Definition 1.3.11.

    Let \(X\) be a variable. The minimum of \(X\) is the lowest value it attains. Similarly, the maximum is the highest value that \(X\) attains. The range of \(X\) is the interval \([\min(X), \max(X)]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Ranges can be computed in R with range(x).
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.12.

    Let \(X\) be a variable. The difference between its third and first quartile is called its inner-quartile range (IQR).
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.13.

    The deviation of a datum \(x\) is its distance from the mean.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.14.

    Let \(X\) be a variable with \(n\) observations. Its variance is the average of the size of all its deviations. When \(X\) comes from a sample, we compute
    \begin{equation*} s^2 = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    When \(X\) represents a population, we can take the mean instead, and we compute
    \begin{equation*} \sigma^2 = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2}{n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Variance for samples can be computed in R with variance(x).
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.15.

    The standard deviation of a variable \(X\) it the square root of its variance.
    🔗
    🔗
    Standard deviations for samples can be computed in R with sd(x).
    🔗
    🔗
